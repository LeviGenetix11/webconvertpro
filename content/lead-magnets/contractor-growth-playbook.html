How to use this playbook

This playbook is written for owners of roofing, landscaping, renovation, plumbing, HVAC, electrical, painting, flooring, deck and fence businesses who want a clearer picture of how homeowners find and choose contractors online, and what to fix first.

Part 1 · Foundations

1. Introduction: your reputation now starts online

Referrals, repeat customers and trucks in the right driveways still matter. But after hearing your name, homeowners look you up: a neighbour's recommendation is usually followed by a search, a look at your photos and reviews, and a website visit before anyone calls.

The truck sign starts curiosity; the smartphone confirms credibility. Your online presence confirms what people heard or raises doubts: a website hard to use on a phone, a Google Business Profile with wrong hours, no recent project photos, or a phone number that differs across sites.

The core shift: word of mouth once generated an immediate call. Today it generates a search. If your digital footprint doesn't instantly corroborate a referral, the lead leaks to a competitor who looks more established online.

The four fundamental pillars

1. Modern websiteFast, mobile-ready, stating what you do, where you operate, and how to get an estimate.
2. Complete Google Business ProfileVerified, with active categories, hours, and regular photo uploads.
3. Unified business informationExact matching name, address and phone number across every directory.
4. Social proofFresh reviews from local customers and authentic project photography, supported by a recognisable brand.

2. How homeowners research contractors

Hiring a contractor requires trust: homeowners let someone into their home, often for weeks, and spend significant money. Every place they check should tell the same accurate story and make the next step obvious.

Search Google or Google MapsService and town terms: "roof repair Whitby," "basement renovation Mississauga," or "landscaper near me."
Compare map pack resultsPhotos, review volume, ratings and recency — outdated feedback or low ratings can rule a company out.
Evaluate two or three websitesServices, service areas, project photos and quote details.
Cross-check social and community inputAsk friends or neighbourhood groups, then search the recommended names for a real logo, working phone number and recent activity.

Two contrasting trade scenarios

Urgent need: roofing after a windstormA homeowner searches "roof leak repair Ajax" on their phone. Show that you serve Ajax, handle emergency repairs and offer tap-to-call.
Planned investment: custom kitchen renovationA couple may spend weeks researching. Show finished kitchens, your process, and how quotes and timelines work.

3. The modern contractor customer journey

Homeowners move through six stages from discovery to a signed contract and completed project. Each transition needs clear information and an easy next step.

4. Common contractor digital marketing mistakes

Most trade businesses lose qualified local jobs not because of poor craftsmanship on site, but because of preventable friction in their digital setup.

  1. Outdated websites displaying projects from years ago, disconnected numbers, or decommissioned services.
  2. No defined service area, forcing homeowners in adjacent towns to guess if you'll travel to their property.
  3. Hidden phone numbers that are static images or unclickable text on mobile.
  4. Bloated quote forms asking for more fields than anyone tests on an actual phone.
  5. Unclaimed Google Business Profiles with inaccurate hours, old photos, or incorrect categories.
  6. Inconsistent business information across directories — mismatched spelling, old addresses, conflicting numbers.
  7. Stock photos of unfamiliar houses instead of genuine local work.
  8. Passive review habits — waiting for happy clients to leave reviews unprompted, or breaking platform rules with incentives.
  9. Bursts of social posting followed by months of silence, creating an impression of inactivity.
  10. Running paid ads too early, driving paid clicks to a site that can't convert visitors.
  11. Zero source tracking, leaving the owner unable to tell which channel produces their best jobs.
Self-check: take three minutes to audit your business against this list. If three or more apply, your digital footprint is actively sending prospects to a competitor. The 30-day action plan (page 18) gives non-technical steps to resolve each one.

5. Why professional websites matter

Your website is the one online asset you fully control. Directories, social platforms and map listings can change their rules and layouts; your website stays yours.

Professional doesn't mean flashy. It means current, fast, easy to read on a phone, accurate and focused on what the homeowner needs to decide.

Confirms referral credibilityLooks established next to competitors after a referral or profile visit.
Pre-qualifies inbound leadsExplains specific services so the right clients reach out and poor-fit projects filter out.
Demonstrates real workmanshipShows real finished work in a project gallery.
Anchors local search visibilityDescribes services and service area fully — the same information Google's own guidance recommends keeping complete and accurate.

Make enquiries easy with click-to-call, a short quote form and clear next steps. A well-built site works around the clock as your primary sales representative.

6. Essential elements of a contractor website

  • Homepage: in one screen, say what you do, where you do it, and how to get a quote.
  • Service pages: one per main service, with inclusions, process, materials or options, and areas served.
  • Service area: the towns and regions you actually serve, named explicitly.
  • Project gallery: real completed-work photos, organised by service.
  • About page: who you are, years of operation, and licences or insurance actually held.
  • Genuine reviews: ideally linked to their real source.
  • Contact page: phone, email, quote form, hours and service area; a physical address only if customers visit.
  • Calls to action and legal pages: a clear next step on every page, plus a privacy policy when collecting form data.

7. Mobile responsiveness

A responsive website adapts its layout to the screen. Google uses the mobile version of a site's content for indexing and ranking, so it should contain the same important content as desktop.

Most urgent local searches — a leaking roof, a flooded basement — happen on a phone, in the moment. If a homeowner has to pinch and zoom to read your phone number, they leave immediately and call the next listing.

Readable and easy to callText readable without zooming, a prominent tap-to-call number, large uncrowded buttons, a one-thumb menu.
Simple formsShort, easy to submit, with the right keyboard — a number pad for phone fields.
Optimized imagesQuick-loading photos that don't overflow the screen or slow the page.
No covering pop-upsNothing that blocks content on a small screen and is hard to close.
Action exercise — the 60-second mobile stress test: on your phone, turn off Wi-Fi, open your website, find your service area and try to submit a mock quote request in under 60 seconds. Note anything that slows you down.

8. Speed and usability

53% of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than three seconds to load. (Google / SOASTA research) For a contractor whose next job might depend on one urgent search, that's a real cost, not a technicality.

Most of what slows a contractor site down is fixable without a developer: oversized photos straight off a phone, unnecessary plugins or page-builder bloat, and third-party scripts (chat widgets, tracking pixels) loaded on every page whether they're needed or not.

Compress images before uploadA photo doesn't need to be 8 megabytes to look sharp on a phone screen.
Fewer plugins, less bloatEvery extra script is one more thing that can slow the page or break.
Test on a real connectionDesktop Wi-Fi hides problems a customer's cellular connection won't.
Static, simple hostingA hand-built, static site has fewer moving parts to slow down or go wrong than a heavy content-management system.

9. Conversion optimization

A visitor who arrives and leaves without contacting you cost you the same marketing effort as one who calls. The average website conversion rate is 2.35%; top performers convert at 5% and above. (WordStream benchmark data) The gap between those numbers is almost always design and clarity, not traffic volume.

  • One primary action per page — don't make a visitor choose between five competing buttons.
  • Say the benefit, not just the task: "Get my free quote," not "Submit."
  • Put the phone number and the main CTA above the fold, every time.
  • Remove anything that isn't helping someone decide to contact you.
  • Put a trust signal (reviews, licence, years in business) near the CTA, not buried at the bottom.

    10. Calls to action

    A call to action is the one instruction you give a visitor: call, request a quote, book a visit. Most contractor sites either bury it or repeat the same generic phrase everywhere.

    Make the phone number tappableA real tel: link, large enough to tap with a thumb, not an image of a phone number.
    Repeat the CTATop of the page, after the services section, and again at the bottom — the same action, every time.
    Match the CTA to the urgency"Call now for emergency service" reads differently than "Book your free estimate."
    Keep the form shortName, phone, what you need, city. Ask for more only after the first contact.

    12. Project galleries and photography

    Photos of real finished work do more to win an estimate than almost anything else on a contractor site — and they're usually the easiest thing to fix, because most owners already have them on their phone.

    Update it regularlyA gallery that stops three years ago reads as a business that's slowed down.

    14. Local SEO fundamentals

    When someone searches "roofer near me" or "kitchen renovation Whitby," Google decides which businesses to show using three main signals.

    Your Google Business ProfileOften the first thing a customer sees, before they ever visit your website.
    Information consistencyMatching name, address and phone across your website and every directory.
    Website relevanceWhether your site clearly names your services and service area.

    Local SEO is the ongoing work of strengthening all three — it isn't a one-time setup you finish and forget. Results vary by location, device and the exact words someone searches; nobody can honestly guarantee a specific ranking.

    15. Google Business Profile

    For most local searches, your profile is seen before your website. An incomplete or unclaimed one is one of the single biggest gaps a local business can have.

    Claim and verify itThrough Google's ownership process if you haven't already.
    Fill in every fieldCategories, hours, services, service area and a clear description.
    Upload photos regularlyRecent project photos, not a one-time batch from years ago.
    Keep hours and information currentWrong hours cost you a visit the moment someone shows up closed.

    16. Reviews and reputation

    Review count and recency are a major factor in who Google shows first, and in who a homeowner actually calls.

    Ask every customerRight after the job, while the experience is fresh — a simple link makes it a 30-second task.
    Respond to every reviewGood and bad. A thoughtful response to a bad review often reassures more than a pile of five-star ones.
    Never buy or incentivize reviewsIt breaks every platform's rules and risks your profile being suspended entirely.

    17. Directory submissions

    Beyond Google, homeowners and Google's own ranking systems both check other directories — general ones like Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, Yelp and Yellow Pages, and trade-specific ones like HomeStars. Being listed, accurately, across the relevant ones is part of looking established.

    Consistency matters more than sheer volume — ten accurate listings beat thirty with mismatched phone numbers.

    Part 3 · Local visibility

    18. Citation consistency

    A "citation" is any place online your business name, address and phone number appear. When they match exactly everywhere, it's one more signal to Google that your business is real and established at that location. When they don't — an old number here, a slightly different business name there — it quietly works against you.

    Pick one canonical format for your name, address and phone number, and fix every listing to match it exactly before adding new ones.

    19. Branding

    A homeowner comparing quotes often judges your visual presentation before they've read a word. A mismatched logo, inconsistent colours or a hand-made business card quietly raises doubt.

    One logo, used everywhereWebsite, truck, uniform and business card — the same mark, not a slightly different version on each.
    A small, deliberate colour paletteTwo to four colours, used consistently, not whatever's closest in the moment.

    Writing honest answers to the questions your customers actually ask — "how much does a basement renovation cost," "how long does a roof replacement take" — earns search traffic and builds trust before the phone ever rings.

    One well-answered question, written in plain language, is worth more than a dozen generic "why choose us" pages.

    Without tracking, it's impossible to know which channel actually produces jobs — and easy to keep spending on the one that merely feels like it does.

    Track every call and formBack to its source — which page, which channel.
    Review it monthly, not yearlySmall, frequent checks catch a broken form fast; a yearly review doesn't.

    23. Budget considerations

    Paid traffic sent to a slow, confusing or incomplete website is money spent proving the problem exists, not fixing it. Fix the foundation first.

    1. Website basics: speed, mobile usability, a working contact form.
    2. Google Business Profile: claimed, complete, and collecting reviews.
    3. Citation consistency across the directories that matter for your trade.
    4. Only then: paid search or social advertising, pointed at a site that can actually convert the clicks.

    24. 30-day action plan

    Week 1 — Website basics

    Make the phone number tappable. Confirm the contact form works end to end. Check the mobile stress test from page 8. Compress your largest photos.

    Week 2 — Google Business Profile and reviews

    Claim or verify your profile. Fill in every field. Upload ten recent project photos. Send a review request to your last five customers.

    Week 3 — Directories and citations

    Pick one canonical name/address/phone format. Check and fix your three most important directory listings against it.

    Week 4 — Branding, content and tracking

    Confirm your logo and colours match across site, truck and cards. Publish one honest answer to a real customer question. Set up call/form tracking so next month is measured, not guessed.
