Most advice on improving conversion rate is written for online stores, not for a business that gets hired after a phone call. If you run a service business, your "conversion" isn't a cart checkout. It's someone calling you or filling out a form instead of clicking back to try the next company in the search results. The average site converts 2.35% of visitors into one of those two things; the better ones hit 5% or higher. Getting from one to the other is usually a handful of specific fixes, not a redesign.

That matters because of what it costs you when it doesn't happen. A contractor paying for ads or ranking well in local search is already winning the hard part: getting a real, in-market visitor to the page. A low conversion rate doesn't mean you need more traffic. It means you're paying (in ad spend, in SEO time, in referrals) to bring people to a door that doesn't open easily. Fix the door first.

Here's an old joke that's still true: if your site still has a visitor counter, a "best viewed in Internet Explorer" note, or an email form that's been "under construction" since a provincial election ago, you don't need a conversion audit. You need a new website. For everyone else, the fixes below apply whether your site is five years old or five months old.

## What counts as a good conversion rate, anyway

Before you change anything, know what you're aiming at. The average website conversion rate across industries is 2.35%. Top performers convert at 5% and above. There's no single "good" number for every trade, but if your site is under 2%, that's a sign something structural is in the way, not that you need to write punchier copy.

The fastest way to find your own number: conversions divided by visitors, times 100. If Google Analytics isn't set up yet, that's step zero. You can't improve a number you're not tracking.

## Start with your actual conversion event

A lot of conversion advice is written for online stores, where the conversion event is "completed checkout." Yours is simpler and, in most cases, there are only two that matter: a phone call and a form submission. Decide which one matters more for your business before you touch anything else.

If most of your jobs come from people calling while they're still standing in front of the problem (a leak, a breaker that tripped, a quote they need today), design the page around the phone number. If your work is more considered, a kitchen reno, a new roof, a quote someone's going to compare against two others, the form matters more because it lets them reach out without picking up the phone. Most pages try to do both and end up prioritizing neither.

This decision should shape the whole page, not just one button. A phone-first emergency service page puts the number in the header, in the hero, and in a sticky mobile bar, and keeps the form short and optional. A form-first renovation page can afford a slightly longer page with project photos and FAQs, because that visitor is building a case for hiring you, not looking for the fastest way to reach a human in the next ten minutes.

![Clean website homepage with a clear call-to-action button](/assets/img/blog/how-to-improve-website-conversion-rate/next-step-obvious.jpg)

## Make the next step obvious in the first screen

A visitor should know what you do, where you work, and what to click within a few seconds, before they scroll. That means a headline that names the service and the city ("Kitchen and basement renovations in Durham Region" beats "Welcome to our website"), one clear button, and nothing competing with it for attention.

Pick one primary action per page and repeat it. If your hero button says "Get a free quote," your sticky header button shouldn't say "Contact us" and your footer shouldn't say "Reach out." Different words for the same action make a visitor pause to figure out if they're actually the same thing. They're not thinking that hard about your site; they're deciding in about two seconds whether to stay.

![Person tapping a phone number to call a business directly](/assets/img/blog/how-to-improve-website-conversion-rate/phone-one-tap.jpg)

## Get people to your phone number in one tap

This is the one most generic conversion-rate guides skip entirely, because they're written for e-commerce. For a service business, the phone number is often the highest-converting thing on the page, and it needs to work like a phone number, not like text that happens to contain digits.

Three things to check right now:
- Is your phone number a real `tel:` link, or just text someone has to copy and dial manually?
- Is it visible without scrolling, on mobile, not just in the footer?
- Does it appear more than once? Header, hero, and a sticky mobile bar covers most of a visit.

If someone has to hunt for your number or your contact form is the only way to reach you, you're losing the exact visitor who was ready to call.

![Simple online contact form filled out on a tablet](/assets/img/blog/how-to-improve-website-conversion-rate/cut-form-fields.jpg)

## Cut your form down to the fields you actually need

Every field you add is a reason for someone to stop. Name, contact info, what they need, and roughly where they are is usually enough to start a conversation. You can get the rest on the phone call.

Look at your current form and ask, for each field: would we actually not call this person back if they left it blank? If the honest answer is no, cut it. A short form that gets submitted beats a detailed one that gets abandoned halfway through.

![Smartphone screen showing a fast-loading website](/assets/img/blog/how-to-improve-website-conversion-rate/load-speed-mobile.jpg)

## Fix load speed and mobile before anything else

53% of mobile visits are abandoned once a page takes longer than 3 seconds to load. That's not a visitor deciding you're not a good fit. That's a visitor never seeing your page at all. No amount of better copy or a stronger call to action fixes a page nobody waited around for.

Run your site through [Google PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) and look at the mobile score specifically, not desktop. Heavy page builders, unoptimized images, and third-party embeds (chat widgets, booking calendars, tracking scripts you added once and forgot about) are the usual culprits. A site built as static pages, without a page builder sitting between your content and the browser, generally doesn't have this problem to begin with.

![Five-star customer review shown on a screen](/assets/img/blog/how-to-improve-website-conversion-rate/trust-proof.jpg)

## Show proof a real local business would trust

75% of people judge a business's credibility based on its website design alone, before they've spoken to anyone. For a service business, that credibility check often comes down to a handful of specific signals: real reviews (not a generic "5-star rated!" badge with no reviews attached), real photos of your actual work, and a service area that names real cities instead of "serving the GTA and beyond."

If you don't have reviews yet, don't fake them. Lead with something true instead: a clear guarantee, a straightforward price, or the offer to see the finished site before paying for it. A specific, honest claim beats a vague confident one every time.

## Your Google Business Profile is already part of the funnel

For a local search, a lot of people never make it to your website at all. They see your [Google Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/) in the map results, check your reviews and hours, and call straight from that listing. If your profile is thin, unclaimed, or has an inconsistent phone number compared to your site, you're losing conversions before your conversion-rate tracking ever sees the visit.

Treat your profile as a second homepage: accurate categories and services, real photos, and a review-request habit after every job. See our [Google Business Profile management](/google-business-profile/) page for the full checklist if you want to go deeper than the basics.

## Watch where people actually drop off, then fix just that

Once the basics above are handled, the fastest remaining gains come from watching real behaviour instead of guessing. Google Analytics 4 will show you which pages people land on and leave from without converting. A heatmap or session-recording tool (several have free tiers) shows you where people stop scrolling or hover over something that isn't actually clickable.

Fix the single biggest drop-off point, wait a few weeks for enough visits to tell you something, then move to the next one. Changing five things at once means you'll never know which one actually mattered.

## Test one change at a time, not everything at once

Once you're past the obvious fixes (speed, a findable phone number, a clear next step), the remaining gains come from testing, not guessing. A real A/B test needs a lot of traffic to mean anything, more than most local service sites get in a month, so a full split-test tool is often overkill. A simpler version works almost as well: change one thing, leave it for two to four weeks, and compare the conversion rate to the same period before the change.

Good candidates to test one at a time:
- The headline on your homepage or main service page.
- The colour or wording of your primary button.
- Whether the phone number or the form gets the top position.
- How many fields are on your quote form.

Resist changing the headline, the button, and the form all in the same week. If the number moves, you won't know which change moved it, and you'll have learned nothing you can reuse on the next page.

Also worth tracking: not just how many people convert, but what happens to the leads afterward. A page that generates twenty form fills a month that never turn into booked jobs isn't actually converting well, it's just collecting names. If you can, tag which pages your actual paying customers came from, not just which pages got the most clicks.

## A two-minute audit you can run on your own site right now

Before you change anything based on this guide, spend two minutes checking where you actually stand:

1. Open your site on your phone, on mobile data, not wifi. Time how long it takes to load.
2. Try to call your own business by tapping the phone number on your homepage. Does it dial, or does it just highlight the text?
3. Count how many fields are on your main contact form.
4. Scroll your homepage and time how long it takes to reach something that looks like proof (a review, a photo of real work, a specific guarantee).
5. Search your own business name on Google and look at what shows up next to the map pin. Is it accurate?

Most sites fail at least one of these. Whichever one yours fails worst is where to start, not wherever feels most exciting to redesign.
